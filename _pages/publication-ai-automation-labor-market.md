<p class="publication-summary__lede">
Artificial intelligence does not enter a labour market as a neutral technical shock. Its effects depend on the tasks people perform, the skills workers already possess, the sectors in which firms operate, and the institutions that determine whether technological change substitutes for labour or augments it. This study compares <strong>Poland and Türkiye</strong> to ask how similar pressures from AI and automation interact with different economic, educational, and institutional structures.

## The central question

Debates about AI and employment are often framed as a binary question: **will machines replace people?** The paper argues that this framing is too coarse.

Jobs are bundles of tasks. Some tasks are routine and predictable; others depend on creativity, judgement, social interaction, or domain expertise. AI can therefore replace one part of a job while simultaneously increasing the value of another.

The analysis is organised around three questions:

1. How does artificial intelligence affect employment structures in Poland and Türkiye?
2. How do skill transformation and labour-market adaptation differ between the two countries?
3. What economic and social consequences follow from automation in each context?

## Four theoretical lenses

The paper combines several strands of labour-economics theory rather than treating automation risk as a single number.

### Task-based and routine-biased technological change

The task-based view models occupations as combinations of activities that differ in their susceptibility to automation. Routine cognitive and manual tasks are easier to codify, while non-routine analytical, creative, and interpersonal tasks are more resilient.

This framework explains why automation can hollow out parts of the middle of the occupational distribution rather than simply reducing employment everywhere.

### Skill-biased technological change

Skill-biased technological change predicts that new technologies can disproportionately raise demand for highly educated and technically capable workers. Without sufficiently rapid expansion of education and training, the resulting productivity gains may therefore coexist with wider wage inequality.

### Labour substitution versus labour augmentation

A crucial distinction is whether AI is used to **replace a human task** or to **increase the productivity of a worker performing that task**. The same underlying technology can produce very different employment outcomes depending on organisational choices.

### Institutions shape the result

Technology does not determine the labour-market outcome on its own. Education systems, labour-market institutions, firm-level training, redistribution mechanisms, and the direction of innovation influence who can use AI productively and who bears the adjustment cost.

## Automation potential is not the same as job loss

The paper contrasts occupation-level automation estimates with more granular task-based interpretations. Earlier occupation-level studies produced high estimates of automation exposure, while later task-level analyses showed that only a subset of activities within most jobs is actually automatable.

The important point is not that a country has a single fixed percentage of jobs that will disappear. Automation potential is mediated by sectoral composition, task design, investment, skills, and organisational adoption.

## Poland and Türkiye start from different labour-market structures

The comparison becomes clearer when employment is separated by skill level.

Poland shows a visible shift toward high-skill employment between 2020 and 2024, alongside a reduction in the share of medium- and low-skill work. Türkiye changes more slowly: medium-skill employment remains dominant and the high-skill share rises only modestly.

That difference matters because AI exposure and AI readiness are not the same thing. A labour market can contain many automatable tasks while lacking the digital and cognitive skills required to capture the productivity gains from automation.

The paper therefore interprets Poland's main challenge as upgrading and restructuring increasingly service- and knowledge-intensive employment, while Türkiye faces the additional challenge of strengthening foundational and digital capabilities across a broader share of the workforce.

## Job destruction and job creation happen together

The study does not treat AI as a one-directional displacement force.

Drawing on the *Future of Jobs Report 2025*, it notes that the next wave of technological transformation is expected to create and displace jobs simultaneously. The labour market therefore experiences **churn**: some roles contract, some expand, and many are redesigned around a different division of labour between humans and machines.

This is why the most useful unit of analysis is often the **task**, not the occupation title.

## Training determines how much productivity is actually realised

Technology access alone is not enough.

The study highlights evidence that AI use inside organisations is already widespread while structured training remains far less common. When employees are expected to adopt AI without adequate support, potential productivity gains are lost and workers experience greater workload pressure, skill anxiety, and uncertainty about professional expertise.

This creates a second digital divide: not merely between people who have access to AI and people who do not, but between organisations that can **integrate AI into learning and job design** and those that merely deploy tools.

## The distributional problem

Even when aggregate employment grows, the gains do not have to be distributed evenly.

Workers whose tasks are complementary to AI may gain productivity and bargaining power. Workers concentrated in routine activities may face wage pressure or occupational displacement. Unequal access to training can reinforce gender, educational, regional, and generational inequalities already present in the labour market.

The paper therefore treats AI policy as a question of **distribution as well as productivity**.

The paper argues that the hybrid trajectory is the most plausible near-term outcome for both countries unless policy and training systems improve significantly.

## What policy can change

The recommendations follow directly from the comparison:

- build **lifelong-learning and reskilling systems** rather than relying only on one-time formal education;
- strengthen foundational literacy, numeracy, digital skills, AI literacy, data reasoning, and cybersecurity capabilities;
- use micro-credentials and modular training to make reskilling accessible to workers already in employment;
- encourage firms to redesign jobs around automatable and augmentable tasks rather than treating AI as a stand-alone software purchase;
- establish clear organisational AI governance, including usage rules, risk assessment, data handling, and transparent decision processes;
- target support toward demographic, occupational, and regional groups with higher exposure and weaker access to training.

## Takeaway

The paper's main conclusion is not that AI inevitably destroys jobs or inevitably creates prosperity.

**The labour-market outcome is institutionally produced.**

AI can substitute for human labour, complement it, or reorganise it. Which path dominates depends on how quickly skills, education, firms, and public policy adapt. Poland and Türkiye face the same technological wave, but they enter it with different labour-market structures and human-capital constraints.

The future of work therefore depends less on whether AI becomes more capable than on whether institutions can ensure that workers remain capable of moving with it.
